# Navigation and RNAV

Understanding how an aircraft navigates helps controllers anticipate what a pilot is able to fly and when vectors may be appropriate.

## RNAV

**RNAV** (Area Navigation) allows an aircraft to navigate between defined points without requiring the aircraft to fly directly over a ground-based navigation facility on every leg. In modern operations, RNAV commonly uses GNSS/GPS and aircraft flight-management systems.

For controller training, remember that RNAV describes the navigation capability and procedure—not simply a single piece of equipment.

## GPS / GNSS

GNSS is the broader satellite-navigation system family; GPS is one GNSS constellation. A pilot's equipment suffix and aircraft capabilities determine which procedures and routes can be accepted.

## VOR

VOR is a ground-based navigation system. Aircraft can use VOR information to establish a course, while controllers may also use conventional procedures that reference VORs.

## NDB

NDBs provide non-directional radio beacons. An aircraft's ADF can use an NDB as a navigation reference. NDB procedures are less common than RNAV procedures but remain useful to understand when reading charts and procedures.

## Pilot Navigation vs. Radar Vectors

A useful controller distinction is:

- **Pilot navigation:** The aircraft navigates itself along the published route or procedure.
- **Radar vectors:** ATC assigns headings to guide the aircraft toward a fix, course, or final approach.
- **VOR / conventional navigation:** A hybrid case where the aircraft uses a ground-based navigation reference while ATC manages the traffic flow.

When vectors are used, the controller is responsible for providing the headings and subsequent instructions needed to connect the aircraft to the intended route or approach, subject to the applicable rules and facility procedures.

## Controller Training Cue

Before issuing an instruction, identify whether the aircraft is expected to:

1. Fly a published RNAV or conventional procedure.
2. Navigate to a fix using its own equipment.
3. Accept radar vectors from ATC.
4. Transition from vectors back to a published procedure.

This distinction is especially useful when reviewing departures, arrivals, approaches, and amendments.
